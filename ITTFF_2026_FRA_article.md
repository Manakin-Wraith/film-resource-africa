# The Plumbing Gap

## Why Africa's Film Boom Needs a Translation Layer, Not Another Platform

**By Gerhard Mostert, Founder, Film Resource Africa**
*Published in the ITTFF 2026 brochure, distributed at the festival (Johannesburg, 3–5 June 2026) and across the CineTour Circuit.*

---

There is a story the African film industry has told itself for two decades: that its problem is a lack of money, a lack of buyers, or a lack of recognition. In May 2026, that story no longer fits the data. The money is arriving. The buyers are looking. The recognition is here.

What is missing is the plumbing.

### The boom is real — and so are the numbers

Africa's film and audiovisual sector contributed over **US$4 billion** to the continent's economy in 2025.[^1] Three African titles were selected for the 2026 Cannes Un Certain Regard line-up.[^2] South Africa's OTT market alone is projected to grow from R4.2 billion in 2024 to R5.6 billion by 2029.[^3] Afreximbank has launched a **US$1 billion Africa Film Fund**.[^4] The Next Narrative Africa Fund has committed US$50 million to content creators on top of that.[^5]

And the demand side is no longer a feeling — it is measured. A study by Parrot Analytics for the Next Narrative Africa Fund found that non-English African storytelling now accounts for **28% of global demand but only 16% of available supply**.[^6] The United States is the single largest external market for African content, generating 8.7% of total demand. African content over-indexes in the Caribbean (more than 60% of demand) and in diaspora-driven European markets such as Belgium and Portugal.[^6][^7]

That mismatch — 28% demand, 16% supply — is a number every African producer, financier, and policy maker should commit to memory. It is the single most important data point in our industry.

### The boom will stall without rails

It will stall because the infrastructure to convert demand into deals, and capital into completed pictures, does not yet exist at scale.

UNESCO estimates that **fewer than 10% of African films secure meaningful international distribution**.[^8] Industry trackers identified between 60 and 80 African titles that premiered at major international festivals between 2022 and 2025 and received no theatrical or streaming release in their countries of origin.[^9] About half of the continent's screen-sector activity remains, in Akoroko's phrase, "functionally invisible."[^9]

The era of foreign streamers solving this for us is over. Variety's coverage of the 2026 Joburg Film Festival declared "the end of Africa's streaming wars."[^10] Netflix has slowed acquisitions in Nigeria and Kenya and is geo-locking more African originals to African territories — cutting global reach precisely when global demand is rising.[^11] Moses Babatope, who launched Lagos-based Nile Media Entertainment Group in 2024, framed this as a "reset" — a forced course-correction away from over-reliance on foreign-owned platforms.[^12]

The most quoted line in this year's discourse, from the *Canary Compass* analysis of the African content economy, captures the diagnosis precisely:

> *"African content travels globally, African payment does not… This is not a quality gap. It is a plumbing gap."*[^7]

That is the right name for what we are facing.

### Why building more platforms will not close the gap

The instinct, when a market is broken, is to build another platform. And the continent has responded with energy: AFRIFF launched its Film and Content Market (AFCM) to host one-on-one deal meetings.[^13] JBX in Johannesburg now draws over 1,000 participants and runs pitching sessions with international buyers.[^14] Nomadic Film Space launched at the Cannes Marché du Film in May 2026, taking African producers on the road to meet private capital.[^15] TIFF: The Market launches in September 2026 with CAD 23 million in Canadian government backing.[^16] Filmhouse Group has articulated a three-layer strategy that is now widely cited: dominate locally, standardise to global expectations, integrate globally.[^17]

Every one of these is doing real work. Every one of them is also episodic.

AFCM and JBX matchmake during festivals. Nomadic matches on the road. Capital sources fund without distribution rails. Streaming platforms aggregate buy-side, with their own commercial interests in the catalogue. None of them, by design, can be what the industry now needs: an **always-on, sector-spanning, sell-side neutral intermediary** that makes African supply legible to global demand on every business day of the year — not just during the two weeks of a festival.

That layer does not yet exist. That is the white space.

### The translation layer

Call the missing piece what it is: a translation layer.

A translation layer is what allows global capital markets to function — the network of brokers, indices, custodians, and clearing houses that turn willing buyers and willing sellers into completed transactions. It is what allows fashion buyers in Milan to source from manufacturers in Tirupur, or what allows a Korean drama to land on a Mexican streaming service the same week it airs in Seoul. It is rarely glamorous. It is almost always indispensable.

Africa's film industry has supply (a generation of filmmaker-founders such as Tendeka Matatu, Mo Abudu, Steven Markovitz, and Moses Babatope are scaling production capacity).[^18] It has demand (28% of it, globally). It has capital (over US$1 billion now committed via Afreximbank alone).[^4] What it does not have is the connective tissue that lets these three find each other reliably.

A translation layer for African film must do five things at once:

1. **Make supply legible.** Catalogue the continent's projects, IP, talent, locations, and crews in formats that global buyers and financiers can actually parse.
2. **Make demand visible.** Surface what international buyers, broadcasters, festivals, and brands are actively seeking — before the briefs harden into closed-door deals.
3. **Be continuous, not episodic.** Operate every day, not only during festival weeks.
4. **Be sell-side neutral.** Represent the continent's interests, not a single platform's catalogue strategy.
5. **Lower transaction cost.** Cut the time from a financier's interest to a producer's signed term sheet from months to weeks.

This is what we are building at Film Resource Africa.

### What this means for the people in the room

To **filmmakers and producers**: stop optimising for festival visibility alone. Visibility without distribution rails is a vanity metric. Build your projects to be findable, financeable, and contractually clean — and use the layer that connects you to demand.

To **financiers, broadcasters, and international media**: the arbitrage is real. Non-English African storytelling is mispriced because it is mis-routed. The first capital that pairs with the right plumbing will compound faster than the second.

To **the industry's institutions**: festivals, funds, and markets remain essential — but they are nodes, not the network. The continent now needs the wiring between them.

The streamer-led decade taught us what foreign platforms will and will not do for African cinema. The next decade will be defined by the rails Africans build for themselves.

The plumbing gap is the most valuable problem in African film today. Closing it is what Film Resource Africa exists to do — and ITTFF, the CineTour Circuit, and the conversations on this festival floor are where it begins.
